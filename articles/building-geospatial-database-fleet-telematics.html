Building a Geospatial Database for Real-Time Fleet Tracking with PostgreSQL, PostGIS and Redis

Nov 23, 2026 · Erick Mulama Shituku · Geospatial Engineering

Introduction

Every day, transport companies rely on trucks to move goods between cities, across borders, and to their final destinations. Managing these movements requires knowing where vehicles are, whether they are following their assigned routes, how long they spend at different locations, and whether deliveries are progressing as expected.

GPS tracking technology makes it possible to monitor vehicles remotely. Small tracking devices installed in vehicles regularly transmit information such as their location, speed, direction of travel, and engine status.

However, collecting this information is only the beginning.

A transport company operating dozens or hundreds of trucks can receive thousands of GPS updates every hour. All this information must be received, stored, and processed before it can be used to monitor vehicles, reconstruct journeys, detect delays, or identify unusual movement.

This creates an interesting challenge: How do you build a database that can continuously receive vehicle tracking data while remaining fast enough to support live monitoring and geographical analysis?

This is one of the engineering problems I have been working on while developing FleetPulse.

What is FleetPulse?

FleetPulse is a fleet management and operations platform that I am developing to help transport and logistics companies manage their vehicles, drivers, trips, and daily operations from one system.

The project grew from my experience working with fleet tracking and transport operations in East Africa, particularly along the Tanzania–Rwanda transport corridor.

During this work, I was involved in monitoring truck movements, following up on delays, coordinating with drivers, and tracking vehicles as they travelled between ports, border crossings, and delivery destinations.

One challenge I observed was that knowing a truck's location did not always provide enough information to understand what was happening operationally.

A GPS tracking platform might show that a truck has stopped near a border crossing. But that information alone does not explain whether the driver is waiting for customs clearance, experiencing a mechanical problem, taking a scheduled rest, or facing another delay.

To understand the situation, someone often needs to combine tracking information with trip records, driver communication, and other operational details.

FleetPulse is intended to bring these different sources of information together.

The platform is being developed to support vehicle tracking, trip management, route monitoring, driver records, maintenance activities, operational reporting, and other fleet management functions.

Rather than functioning only as a map displaying moving vehicles, the aim is to connect vehicle movements with the activities and decisions involved in running a transport business.

For example, when a truck leaves a depot, FleetPulse should be able to relate its movement to an assigned trip. When it stops unexpectedly, the system should help identify the event and make that information available for operational follow-up.

To make these capabilities possible, the platform needs a reliable way to manage large amounts of location data.

That is where PostgreSQL, PostGIS, and Redis come into the picture.

Why the database matters

At the centre of a GPS tracking system is a continuous stream of information from vehicles.

Imagine a transport company operating 100 trucks, with each truck sending its location every 10 seconds.

Under continuous reporting, the system would receive approximately:

  • 600 location updates every minute
  • 36,000 updates every hour
  • 864,000 updates every day
  • More than 25 million updates in 30 days

These are illustrative figures, not measured FleetPulse traffic. Actual volumes depend on how frequently tracking devices report, network availability, and vehicle activity.

Each update may contain several pieces of information, including the vehicle's location, speed, direction, timestamp, and additional sensor readings.

The system needs to preserve this information because it may be required later to investigate a journey, calculate distances, review driver behaviour, or generate operational reports.

At the same time, users monitoring the fleet need access to the most recent information without waiting for the system to search through millions of historical records.

This creates two different requirements.

The first is reliable storage. The system must maintain an accurate history of vehicle movements and related operational records.

The second is fast access and processing. New information must move through the system efficiently so that vehicle locations and operational events can be displayed or analyzed with minimal unnecessary delay.

These requirements influenced how I designed FleetPulse's data architecture.

Choosing PostgreSQL, PostGIS and Redis

I selected PostgreSQL as the main database for FleetPulse because it provides a reliable way to organize and manage related information.

For example, a transport company may have multiple vehicles, each associated with a tracking device, driver assignments, and a history of trips. PostgreSQL allows these records to be connected while maintaining consistency.

However, ordinary database functionality is not enough for all the geographical operations required by a fleet tracking system.

This is where PostGIS becomes important.

PostGIS is an extension that adds geographical capabilities to PostgreSQL. It allows the database to understand locations, distances, routes, and geographic boundaries.

Instead of treating a vehicle's latitude and longitude as unrelated numbers, PostGIS allows those coordinates to be stored and analyzed as a location on the Earth's surface.

This makes it possible to answer questions such as whether a truck is inside a depot, how close it is to a delivery destination, or whether it has moved outside an assigned route.

I also incorporated Redis into the architecture.

Redis is a fast data storage system that is particularly useful when an application needs quick access to frequently changing information.

In FleetPulse, Redis supports live vehicle position information and event processing, while PostgreSQL remains responsible for persistent data storage.

This distinction is important.

A fleet manager looking at a live map usually needs the latest known position of each vehicle. They do not need the entire movement history to be retrieved every time the map updates.

By separating live information from historical storage, the architecture reduces the need to repeatedly query large historical datasets for information that changes frequently.

The platform also uses dedicated background workers to process telemetry and identify operational events without requiring every calculation to be completed as part of an incoming application request.

Together, these technologies form the foundation of FleetPulse's geospatial data system.

The following sections explain how I structured the database, how GPS information enters the system, how PostGIS supports geographical analysis, and how Redis and background processing help manage the different workloads.

They also examine the performance considerations and engineering trade-offs involved in building a system designed to handle continuous vehicle tracking data.
